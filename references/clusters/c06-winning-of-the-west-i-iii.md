## 1. The thesis: expansion, settlement, and conquest

The opening scale is continental and racial. Begin beyond the immediate campaign: trace the movement of peoples, the carrying of law, speech, and custom, and the pressure of settlers across land held by others. The history does not call the event a peaceful diffusion. It names conquest, colonization, dispossession, migration, and war; the frontier must be settled and conquered.

> “During the past three centuries the spread of the English-speaking peoples over the world's waste spaces has been not only the most striking feature in the world's history, but also the event of all others most far-reaching in its effects and its importance.”

> “The vast movement by which this continent was conquered and peopled cannot be rightly understood if considered solely by itself.”

> “The ground had to be not only settled but conquered, sometimes at the expense of the natives, often at the expense of rival European races.”

The historical verdict is carried in its verbs: Americans “dispossessed,” “won,” “acquired,” “drove out,” “settled,” and “conquered”; tribes resist and retreat; European powers fight for mastery. Do not reduce the movement to official diplomacy.

## 2. The backwoodsmen make the frontier

The causal rule is that the frontier is advanced by the settlers and fighters on it, while the distant government comes afterwards under their pressure. Put the backwoodsman before the statesman. He is hunter, tree-feller, farmer, rifleman, militia captain, and father of a household; his settlement is both home and outpost.

> “The pioneers, though warlike and fond of fighting, were primarily settlers; their soldiering came in as a purely secondary occupation.”

> “The west could never have been conquered, in the teeth of so formidable and ruthless a foe, had it not been for the personal prowess of the pioneers themselves.”

> “No regular army could have done what they did. Only trained woodsmen could have led the white advance into the vast forest-clad regions, out of which so many fair States have been hewn.”

Explain national development from hard, armed settlers’ power to endure, fight, settle, and take possession, not from public plans alone. The government makes treaties; settlers press on; war follows.

## 3. The Southwest and the Revolution in the West

The Southwest is won by people acting in advance of government. In the Northwest the nation’s army goes first and settlers follow its shelter; in Kentucky, Tennessee, and Texas, settlers make their own army, leaders, and policy. Keep the distinction sharp, because it orders the judgment of the early West.

> “The Southwest, including therein what was once called simply the West, and afterwards the Middle West, was won by the people themselves, acting as individuals, or as groups of individuals, who hewed out their own fortunes in advance of any governmental action.”

> “In the Southwest the early settlers acted as their own army, and supplied both leaders and men.”

George Rogers Clark is the standing figure: set him against more famous eastern names and establish that his Illinois campaign decides the later possession of the Northwest.

> “The names of Ethan Allen and Marion are probably better known than is that of George Rogers Clark; yet their deeds, as regards their effects, could no more be compared to his, than his could be compared to Washington's.”

Clark’s men are “hard-working, hard-fighting freemen,” settlers with wives and children. The preferred unit is obstacle, small force, leadership, seizure, and consequence.

## 4. The historical sentence and its hard edge

The R2 sentence is a ledger of cause and result: collective actor, geography and resistance, *but* or a semicolon, then consequence. Questions do not solicit a reader.

> “The warlike borderers who thronged across the Alleghanies, the restless and reckless hunters, the hard, dogged, frontier farmers, by dint of grim tenacity overcame and displaced Indians, French, and Spaniards alike, exactly as, fourteen hundred years before, Saxon and Angle had overcome and displaced the Cymric and Gaelic Celts.”

> “They were led by no one commander; they acted under orders from neither king nor congress; they were not carrying out the plans of any far-sighted leader.”

> “In obedience to the instincts working half blindly within their breasts, spurred ever onwards by the fierce desires of their eager hearts, they made in the wilderness homes for their children, and by so doing wrought out the destinies of a continental nation.”

Use third-person judgments. The historical voice names racial categories, “civilization,” “savage,” mastery, displacement, and conquest as it writes them; it also records “fierce and dogged resistance.” Keep resistance and conquest inside the causal history.
