> **Use:** boyhood, Harvard, the Assembly years, early politics, the Little Missouri ranch, or a dated private record from 1877–1886. Both strands are R1. The autobiography is the retrospective, full-sentence end of the family; the diaries are its private extreme, not a family of their own. Do not turn either into R2 historical narration or R3 domestic correspondence.

---

## The internal gradient: recollection, then the day itself

The autobiography looks backward from mastery without pretending that youth was already mastery. It gives a concrete scene, takes the lesson from it, admits a limitation or mistake, and returns to effort, duty, fitness, or work. The diary is the other pole: date first, then what happened—exercise, study, birds, weather, a person, a defeat, a journey, a political step. Its nouns are particular and its judgments quick.

The diaries are terse. Their 28.48 mean sentence length is a splitter artefact: entries often lack terminal punctuation, so dates, fragments, and editorial matter are dragged together. Read the dated entry as the unit: “Beaten by Dick Grimble, boxing.” “Left for Cambridge.” “Grinding like mad for the semi annuals.”

Do not pool the diary’s telegraphic record with the mature recollection in one passage. For dated private record, use day, weather, act, result; a name or specimen is often enough. For formation, use the memoir’s longer movement: scene, limitation, strenuous correction, plain verdict. Both retain first-person ownership. The diary says what I did; the memoir says what the doing made necessary.

## Make weakness a job of work

The formative move is neither confession for its own sake nor a triumphal fable. Begin with a bodily or practical insufficiency, show the humiliating fact without evasive ornament, and make training the answer. The stubbornness lies in repeated exertion, not in natural genius.

> “Having been a sickly boy, with no natural bodily prowess … I was nervous and timid.”

> “I made up my mind that I must try to learn so that I would not again be put in such a helpless position.”

> “I was a painfully slow and awkward pupil, and certainly worked two or three years before I made any perceptible improvement whatever.”

Boxing, wrestling, riding, walking, shooting, collecting, and ranch labor are means by which nerve, endurance, and useful capacity are acquired. Do not claim a gift that the scene denies. “Any man, if he chooses, can gradually school himself to the requisite nerve”; the qualification matters, for the work yields respectable competence, not an automatic right to boast.

In private record, allow defeat as readily as victory: “Sparred for over a half an hour steadily with Sharon, getting the worst of it of course; but I gave him his work.”

## Affection, discipline, and grief

Boyhood does not write sentimentally about the household. Give objects, rooms, habits, names, and a remembered act of justice before naming the lesson. The father’s authority holds strength, tenderness, work, and moral seriousness together.

> “My father, Theodore Roosevelt, was the best man I ever knew. He combined strength and courage with gentleness, tenderness, and great unselfishness.”

> “With great love and patience, and the most understanding sympathy and consideration, he combined insistence on discipline.”

A key in the latch, the “treasures” in the dressing-table box, a bootjack, or Christmas stockings does more than a general tribute. Then say the verdict: “no one whom I have ever met approached his combination of enjoyment of life and performance of duty.”

The diary does not make private grief a public doctrine. It can abruptly break the day’s record: “O, how little worthy I am of such a father; I feel such a hopeless sense of inferiority to him.” Keep the language particular—father, Christmas, the remembered voice, the household—and let the resolve follow: “with the help of my God I shall try to lead such a life as he would have wished.” Do not make a public peroration from this register.

## Learning, politics, and the ranch: real things first

Education matters when it takes hold of an object, craft, person, or work—birds, scientific names, a naval history, boxing, a ranch task, actual politics. The school exercise that merely demands an assigned argument earns impatience: “What we need is to turn out of our colleges young men with ardent convictions on the side of the right; not young men who can make a good argument for either right or wrong as their interest bids them.”

The Assembly record is nominations, caucuses, factions, votes, platforms, and allies. Put the position in the compact terms of the diary: “My platform is; strong Republican on State matters, but Independent on local and municipal affairs.” Then show a particular contest. The memoir corrects youthful judgment without surrendering the standard: “I was young; there was much in the judgment which I then formed on this matter which I should now revise.”

The ranch pages are horses, weather, cattle, rifles, trails, snow, and named companions. The hard country is not a costume; it is cold, hunger, night riding, labor, and shared competence.

> “In that land we led a free and hardy life, with horse and with rifle.”

> “We knew toil and hardship and hunger and thirst; and we saw men die violent deaths … but we felt the beat of hardy life in our veins, and ours was the glory of work and the joy of living.”

The later voice admires the ranch life but judges its passing a national gain: “It was right and necessary that this life should pass,” because the permanent home-maker makes a durable country. Put personal loss beside public judgment. In the diary, stop with the fact: “Rode down (alone) to the first shack on Elkhorn Ranche; very cold; had to rustle for myself.”

## A working sequence for this period

1. Choose the end of R1: retrospective formation or dated private record.
2. Start with an object or action—gloves, bird, trail, vote, horse, weather—not a disembodied quality.
3. Let failure, difficulty, or duty state the test; answer it with training, work, or a definite decision.
4. In memoir, draw the plain lesson after the scene; in diary, stop when the fact is put down.
5. Keep pride proportioned: exact effort, exact result, room for the joke at one’s own expense.
