# Attested happenings

## Childhood, study, and the private record

### 1. The dough under the kitchen table — childhood (no exact date)

As a four-year-old, Roosevelt bit his elder sister’s arm, ran to the yard conscious that he had committed an offense, fetched dough from the kitchen, and hid under the kitchen table. When his father found him, he threw the dough, fled toward the stairs, and was caught halfway up. He later recorded that the punishment “fitted the crime.”  
*Source: c02, autobiography, childhood recollection.*

### 2. The dead seal on Broadway — childhood (no exact date)

After seeing a dead seal laid out on a market slab on Broadway, Roosevelt returned to the market day after day, measured the animal with a folding foot-rule because he had no tape measure, and began a homemade natural history from those measurements. He later called the measurements “utterly useless” and recalled an unfulfilled wish to preserve the seal.  
*Source: c02, autobiography, childhood recollection.*

### 3. Lost from the night herd — Dakota period, 1880s

On an early night-herd assignment, Roosevelt started in the wrong direction on a pitch-dark night and failed to find either the cattle or the wagon before sunrise. The cowboy whom he was supposed to relieve had stood double guard and greeted him with “withering scorn.”  
*Source: c02, autobiography, Dakota recollection.*

### 4. The Little Missouri stampede — Dakota period, 1880s

During a night storm, lightning struck beside the herd and sent the cattle running. Roosevelt’s horse went over a cut bank into the Little Missouri; horse and rider reached the far side through water and quicksand. By dawn he had brought back part of the herd, found a fellow cowboy on foot after that man’s horse had run into a tree and died, ate a hasty breakfast, and then rode the day’s long circle. He counted nearly forty consecutive hours in the saddle, with five changes of horse.  
*Source: c02, autobiography, Dakota recollection.*

### 5. CR13: correction and shared responsibility — 1913 retrospective

Roosevelt recorded that the gilt malachite carving in his childhood parlor had long seemed a priceless work of art to him; “it was not until I was well in middle age that it occurred to me that I was mistaken.” In his account of the unprepared Army in the Spanish War, he did not assign the failures only to officers suddenly put in command: “the fault of all of us, of you, my reader, and of myself.”  
*Source: c02, autobiography, childhood recollection; c03, autobiography, Spanish War preparation. Verbatim evidence for CR13.*

## Dated diary fragments, 1877–1880

### 6. A boxing loss — February 10, 1877

The Harvard diary entry for February 10 contains a complete account of the occasion in four words: “Beaten by Dick Grimble, boxing.”  
*Source: c09, diary entry, February 10, 1877.*

### 7. First deer and a minimal camp — July 5–7, 1877

At Potter’s Pond on July 5, Roosevelt recorded shooting his first deer, “a buck,” as well as black ducks and ruffed grouse. On leaving the woods two days later, he entered the week’s bag and noted that the party had had no tent; its food had been bread and tea.  
*Source: c09, diary entries, July 5 and July 7, 1877.*

### 8. A small boat in a northeaster — August 25, 1877

Returning from Whitestone in his little boat, Roosevelt was caught in a heavy northeaster and reached home at one o’clock in the morning.  
*Source: c09, diary entry, August 25, 1877.*

### 9. His father’s burial — February 12, 1878

After his father’s death, Roosevelt entered that he had “just been buried.” The entry records the suspense of the journey home, the sight of his father’s dead face, and the sound of the first earth falling on the coffin.  
*Source: c09, diary entry, February 12, 1878.*

### 10. News of Harry Minot — September 4, 1878

George Minot told Roosevelt that Harry Minot had lost his mind and was in an asylum. Roosevelt described Minot as a sweet, gentle, bright, and true friend, and wrote of sorrow for Minot’s family.  
*Source: c09, diary entry, September 4, 1878.*

### 11. Winter rowing through shore ice — December 30, 1878

Roosevelt and Jack Elliott took a rowboat out in snow and wind, spent half an hour breaking through shore ice, launched through heavy rollers, and were soaked through by freezing water. He called the rowing to Rocky Point “the devils own work.”  
*Source: c09, diary entry, December 30, 1878.*

### 12. The overturned sleigh — February 3, 1880

In heavy snow, Roosevelt drove a sleigh to Chestnut Hill. On the way home, the sleigh overturned in a deep drift and dragged him about three hundred yards while he held the reins before he stopped the horse.  
*Source: c09, diary entry, February 3, 1880.*

### 13. Marriage to Alice Hathaway Lee — October 27, 1880

At noon on his twenty-second birthday, Roosevelt married Alice Hathaway Lee. The diary calls her “an ideally beautiful bride” and records the couple’s overnight journey to Springfield.  
*Source: c09, diary entry, October 27, 1880.*

## Legislature, police, and administration

### 14. The chair-leg committee meeting — New York Legislature, early 1880s

When a committee majority repeatedly refused either to report a railroad bill favorably or unfavorably, Roosevelt loosened the leg from a broken chair and placed it beside him out of sight. He put the bill in his pocket and announced that he would report it himself, saying that if challenged on the Assembly floor he would give his reasons for suspecting blackmail. The threatened riot did not occur. The bill then stalled in the Legislature; a railway representative took it from him, and a veteran leader later secured unanimous passage.  
*Source: c02, autobiography, New York Legislature recollection.*

### 15. Otto Raphael at the Bowery institute — Police Commission, 1895–1897

At the Young Men’s Institute in the Bowery, Roosevelt met Otto Raphael, introduced as a young Jewish man who had saved women and children from a burning building. Roosevelt told Raphael to sit the police examination; Raphael passed, was appointed, and became an officer whom Roosevelt described as admirable.  
*Source: c03, autobiography, Police Commission recollection.*

### 16. The overdue promotion of a life-saver — Police Commission; October 21, 1896 follow-up

Roosevelt summoned an aging roundsman after learning that the man had rescued a woman from drowning. The officer had spent twenty-two years on the force, saved about twenty-five people from drowning or fire, held several gallantry medals, and had no political backing. Roosevelt promoted him at once. On October 21, 1896, at age fifty-five, the officer again jumped from a dark dock into cold water and saved a man; Roosevelt identified him as having then saved twenty-nine lives in twenty-three years.  
*Source: c03, autobiography, Police Commission recollection.*

### 17. The sealed order on medical impostors — Police Commission, 1895–1897

At Roosevelt’s direction, each New York precinct received a sealed order not to be opened until just before morning roll call. Every patrolman was then to record the name and address on every doctor’s sign along his beat and return the sheet through the precinct. Within two hours prosecuting officials had the names and addresses of everyone in the city presenting himself as a physician; scores of pretended doctors were prosecuted or driven out.  
*Source: c03, autobiography, Police Commission recollection.*

### 18. The heat week in the tenements — Police Commission, midsummer 1895–1897

During an extreme midsummer heat spell, Roosevelt spent most of each night walking tenement districts and visiting police stations. The Health, Police, and Fire Departments worked to relieve suffering, including flooding the streets; so many horses died that removal capacity failed. One shopkeeper repeatedly complained about a dead horse outside his door. When a dray carrying eleven other dead horses arrived to remove it and broke down, he wrote asking that the city remove either the horses or his shop.  
*Source: c03, autobiography, Police Commission recollection.*

## Cuba, 1898

### 19. The fight whose course he did not know — Las Guásimas, June 1898

In the first fighting near Santiago, Roosevelt wrote that he was trying to get into the fight and to do right once in it while believing that everyone else understood the situation. Later he learned that “pretty much everybody else was as much in the dark” as he was. A troop disappeared in dense jungle after deployment; he feared a court-martial for losing it, retained the last platoon in column, and eventually received command of the left wing after Major Brodie was shot.  
*Source: c03, autobiography, Las Guásimas recollection.*

### 20. Holding the Yucatan gangway — Port Tampa, June 1898

When the transport *Yucatan* arrived, Roosevelt found that it had been assigned to a regular regiment, another volunteer regiment, and his own men, although not all could embark. He held the gangway against the volunteer regiment and delayed the regulars with respectful messages and parleys until he could shout to Leonard Wood aboard the transport. Without knowing Wood’s words, he treated the gesture as an order to keep the gangway; his regiment boarded at the double while part of the regular regiment and the other volunteer regiment went elsewhere.  
*Source: c03, autobiography, embarkation recollection.*

### 21. Eleven hundred pounds of beans — Santiago campaign, July 1898

After the Guásimas fight, Roosevelt learned that food lay on the beach at Siboney and took thirty or forty men down to obtain it. A commissary officer told him beans could be issued only to the officers’ mess. Roosevelt returned with a request for eleven hundred pounds of beans for that mess; the officer objected that the officers could not eat that quantity, and Roosevelt replied that the officer did not know their appetites. The requisition was forwarded and the beans were released, with a warning that the cost might be deducted from Roosevelt’s salary.  
*Source: c03, autobiography, Santiago campaign recollection.*

### 22. Bucky O’Neill’s death — San Juan fighting, July 1, 1898

Before the charge, Bucky O’Neill walked before his men smoking, despite their requests that he take cover. After a sergeant warned that a Spanish bullet would hit him, O’Neill answered that no Spanish bullet had been made that could kill him. A bullet then struck his mouth and passed out the back of his head.  
*Source: c05, *The Rough Riders*, San Juan account.*

### 23. Five men beyond the wire — San Juan fighting, July 1, 1898

After calling for a charge on the next line of trenches, Roosevelt crossed a wire fence and ran forward. About a hundred yards on, he found that only five men had come with him. With bullets striking the grass around them and two men wounded, he told the remaining three to stay and fire while he returned for the brigade. On reaching the crest he angrily reproached the troopers for failing to follow; they told him they had neither heard nor seen him go.  
*Source: c05, *The Rough Riders*, San Juan account.*

### 24. Refugee bundles and infection — Santiago, July 1898

During the siege, refugees passed through the American lines. Roosevelt’s men gave them food from their own limited stores until he forbade it and directed the refugees to headquarters because his regiment’s supplies were insufficient. After the surrender, the men carried bundles and children for returning refugees. When disease appeared among them, he stopped the handling of their bundles; a trooper called Happy Jack protested that the Almighty would not let a man catch disease while he was doing a good action.  
*Source: c05, *The Rough Riders*, Santiago account.*

### 25. Liquor collected on the return voyage — August 1898

On the voyage home, the ship’s captain reported drunken, insubordinate stokers and engineers and attributed the liquor to Roosevelt’s men. Roosevelt announced that surrendered liquor would be returned ashore and that liquor not surrendered would be thrown overboard. About seventy flasks and bottles were handed over; he found and threw overboard about twenty more. He sent a detail to watch the stokers and engineers under the chief engineer, after which the drunkenness stopped.  
*Source: c05, *The Rough Riders*, return-voyage account.*
