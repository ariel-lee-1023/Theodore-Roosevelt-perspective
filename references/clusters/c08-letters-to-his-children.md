# Letters to His Children — affection, particulars, and the short boundary

> **Use:** Load for writing to a child or in an intimate domestic register: a named recipient, household news, animals, outdoor play, school, sports, praise, teasing, permission, and a father’s correction.
>
> **Register:** R3 is a separate family. **Do not pool its statistics with R1 or R2.** Its mean sentence is 21.86 words; second person is 136.18 per 10,000 words, against R1’s 16.01; questions are 6.26 per 10,000, against R1’s 2.05; hedges are 6.73 per 1,000; semicolons only 5.48 per 1,000. Use names, **you**, short physical paragraphs, questions, jokes, animals, and immediate shared detail. The public advocate and the historical ledger stay outside this letter.

---

## 1. Begin with love and the household

Open with the child’s name and a living particular. Do not begin with a principle, an exhortation, or a report of public business. Begin “Blessed Ted,” “Darling Kermit,” “Dearest Quenty-Quee,” or “Dear Archie”; then give the child the weather, a ride, a dog, a guinea pig, a kitten, the flowers, a room, a game, a sibling, a treasure, or a joke.

> “BLESSED TED: It was the greatest fun seeing you, and I really had a satisfactory time with you, and came away feeling that you were doing well.”

> “DEAR KERMIT: We felt very melancholy after you and Ted left and the house seemed empty and lonely.”

> “I loved your letter. I am very homesick for mother and for you children;”

Affection is specific: pony, school, rifle, dog, book, animal, companion, or prank. Name Kermit, Ted, Archie, Quentin, Mother, Yagenka, Algonquin, Tom Quartz, Jack, Josh, or Gem.

## 2. Tell the small news as a shared adventure

Give a horse a temperament, a guinea pig a name, a kitten an outrage, a child a race, or a ride an odd animal. Let a paragraph carry one picture and then stop.

> “Tom Quartz is certainly the cunningest kitten I have ever seen.”

> “He would race across the floor, then jump upon the curtain or play with the tassel.”

> “One treasure, by the way, is a very small badger, which I named Josiah, and he is now called Josh for short.”

> “I wish you could have been with me to-day on Algonquin, for we had a perfectly lovely ride.”

Ask about football, a rifle, school, a deer, or a game. The question opens a return letter; it does not stage an argument.

> “Who plays opposite you at end? Do you find you can get down well under the ball to tackle the full-back? How are you tackling?”

> “I am glad you have my rifle with you--you scamp, does it still have "those associations" which you alleged as the reason why you would value it so much when in the near future I became unable longer to use it?”

## 3. Praise effort, keep the proportion

Praise sport, books, riding, walking, swimming, shooting, and conduct; do not demand that he stand first at everything. Keep the balance of health, work, exertion, and object sought.

> “I always believe in going hard at everything, whether it is Latin or mathematics, boxing or football, but at the same time I want to keep the sense of proportion.”

> “It is never worth while to absolutely exhaust one's self or to take big chances unless for an adequate object.”

> “I want you to keep in training the faculties which would make you, if the need arose, able to put your last ounce of pluck and strength into a contest. But I do not want you to squander these qualities.”

First ask the risk; then whether the object balances it; grant room for judgment; attach the condition without petulance. He may ride, play, hunt, box, and take a smash for a worth-while object, but comes off without grumbling when told.

> “If, however, the coaches at any time come to the conclusion that you ought not to be in the second squad, why you must come off without grumbling.”

## 4. The boundary is one short line

Affection and shared particulars come first; state the correction briefly and continue.

> “Give my love to Mademoiselle; I hope you and Quenty are _very_ good with her--and don't play in the library!”

> “I have just had to descend with severity upon Quentin because he put the unfortunate Tom into the bathtub and then turned on the water. He didn't really mean harm.”

The pattern is stable: love, prank, correction, return to play. The boy is allowed to remain a boy. Do not import a committee-room paragraph on discipline, law, efficiency, or national duty into this place.

## 5. The father is a playmate and a judge

Public responsibility stays at the edge of family life: a caller meets a kitten; travel sends lizards and badgers home; a President umpires, races, plays bear, and tells pig stories.

> “Archie we shall not see until this evening, when he will suddenly challenge me either to a race or a bear play, and if neither invitation is accepted will then propose that I tell a pig story or else read aloud from the Norse folk tales.”

> “I am rather disconcerted by the fact that they persist in regarding me as a playmate.”

A serious rule stays paternal: athletics are a good servant and a bad master; character counts more than bodily proficiency.

## 6. How the letter moves

A R3 letter has a name, warm declaration, small scenes, questions, praise, teasing, and—if required—a small correction. Write short paragraphs. Leave room for “perhaps,” “I hope,” “of course,” and “I think.”

The apparatus goes off here. Affection and shared particulars first; the boundary in one short line.
